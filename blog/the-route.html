04 — Conversion path

The route

The fourth layer · 4 min read

In short: Every page has to end somewhere on purpose.

A visitor who reaches the bottom of your page with nothing obvious to do has been failed by this layer. So has the business owner who has no idea how many of them there were.

What abandonment looks like when it is measured

The most thorough public research on people giving up online comes from Baymard Institute, who have run 25 rounds of qualitative usability testing across more than 4,400 test sessions and benchmarked 344 leading e-commerce sites.

That figure is from e-commerce checkouts, not from plumbers' enquiry forms, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What transfers is the mechanism rather than the number: people abandon journeys when the next step is unclear, effortful, or asks for more than they are ready to give.

The route on a service site

A local business site usually has one or two things it needs a visitor to do. Ring, or send an enquiry. The route layer is everything that makes those inevitable rather than possible:

  • A single primary action, repeated, rather than five competing ones
  • The phone number visible without scrolling, and tappable
  • Forms that ask for the minimum needed to reply — usually a name and one contact detail
  • Plain language on the button that says what happens next
  • Confirmation that something actually sent, and what to expect afterwards
What it costs you

Traffic with nowhere to go, and no way to count it. Without tracking on calls and form submissions you cannot tell a page that earns its keep from one that is decoration, so every future decision is a guess.

Measure it or it is not a route

The half of this layer that gets skipped is the counting. If a tap on your phone number is not recorded as an event, and a form submission is not recorded as a conversion, then you have a website that might be working and no way to find out.

This is the least glamorous layer and the one that turns the rest into a business case. It is also the reason to build it in from the start: retrofitting tracking onto a site that has been live for a year gives you a year of nothing to compare against.

Sources

  1. Baymard Institute — E-Commerce Cart & Checkout Usability research programme (25 rounds of usability testing, 4,400+ sessions, 344 benchmarked sites). https://baymard.com/research/checkout-usability
