What is the European Accessibility Act?

The European Accessibility Act is an EU directive that sets common accessibility requirements for certain products and services, so that people with disabilities can use them more easily. It became law in Ireland on 28 June 2025, through S.I. No. 636 of 2023 — the European Union (Accessibility Requirements of Products and Services) Regulations, which transposes the EU directive. The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) is the authority responsible for enforcing it here.

Like most EU rules of this kind, it wasn't written with a one-person clinic or a two-van trades business in mind — it's aimed primarily at larger organisations. That's exactly why the Act includes a specific carve-out for the smallest businesses, and it's worth knowing exactly what that carve-out does and doesn't cover.

Does the microenterprise exemption cover your business?

Most likely, yes — if you're providing a service. The CCPC defines a microenterprise as a business with fewer than 10 people, and an annual turnover or balance sheet total not exceeding €2 million. Its guidance states plainly: "the requirements and obligations of the EAA do not apply to microenterprises providing services." A huge share of the owner-operator clinics, salons, trades businesses and shops we build websites for in Tipperary and beyond fall well inside that threshold.

That exemption is genuinely useful, and it's worth saying so clearly rather than using the Act to sell fear. Most small Irish businesses reading this will not have a legal obligation here.

What's the catch — why doesn't this apply to everything?

The exemption only covers services, not products. If your business manufactures, imports or distributes a product the Act covers — certain electronic devices or self-service terminals, for example — the accessibility requirements still apply to that product, even if you're a microenterprise. A general small-business website, which is delivering information and a way to get in touch or book, sits on the services side of that line. If your business also sells a physical product that's specifically named under the Act, that's a separate question worth checking properly rather than assuming the same exemption covers it.

"Most of the small businesses I talk to hear 'accessibility law' and brace for a big compliance job. For the vast majority, the honest answer is: you're exempt, and it's a lot simpler than it sounds. The bit worth caring about isn't the legal threshold — it's that a website which works properly for everyone tends to be a better website, full stop."

If you're exempt, why bother with accessibility at all?

Because the things accessibility asks for — readable text, decent colour contrast, a site that works with a keyboard as well as a mouse, alt text on images — are also just good practice. They tend to overlap with the same choices that make a site easier to read on a phone, quicker to scan, and clearer to whoever's landing on it, whether that's a customer skimming on their lunch break or someone using a screen reader. If you're weighing up a rebuild anyway, our piece on the signs your website needs a redesign covers a lot of the same ground from a different angle.

It's also worth remembering the €2 million turnover threshold isn't a fixed ceiling for your business forever. A business that grows past microenterprise size at some point stops being exempt, so building with reasonably accessible habits from the start means there's no scramble to catch up later.

What does the underlying accessibility standard actually ask for?

Where the Act does apply, the technical benchmark used across the EU is EN 301 549, the harmonised standard for ICT accessibility, which draws on the same principles as WCAG 2.1 AA — things like sufficient colour contrast, text that can be resized without breaking the layout, forms with proper labels, and content that doesn't rely on colour alone to convey meaning. None of that is exotic. It's the same set of habits that tends to come bundled into a well-built, modern site, which is one reason keeping a site properly maintained after launch matters as much as building it well in the first place — standards and expectations move on, even when the strict legal deadline doesn't apply to you.

If you're not sure whether your specific setup — a booking widget, a payment page, a product you sell — falls inside or outside the exemption, that's exactly the kind of detail worth checking with the CCPC or a professional adviser rather than guessing, and it's also worth revisiting if you're comparing one-time and monthly website plans, since ongoing care is where these details usually get caught early rather than found the hard way.

Frequently asked questions

Does the Accessibility Act apply to my small business website?

If you're a microenterprise providing a service — fewer than 10 people and turnover or balance sheet under €2 million — the CCPC's guidance says the Act's requirements do not apply to you. Products are treated differently, so check which one applies to what you do.

What counts as a microenterprise under the Act?

A business with fewer than 10 employees and an annual turnover or balance sheet total not exceeding €2 million. Most owner-operator businesses in Ireland fit this definition comfortably.

If I'm exempt, do I need to do anything at all?

Legally, no — but accessible design tends to be better design for everyone, and it's worth doing as your site is built or updated rather than treating it as a compliance project.
