Irish rules & compliance

What an Irish dentist can and can't put on their website

17 August 2026 · 6 min read · by Luke Stapleton

TL;DR: The Dental Council of Ireland regulates the profession and publishes codes of practice covering conduct, fee display and cosmetic treatments. This article points you to the right places to check before you publish — it isn't a substitute for reading the Council's own current codes.
PointDetail

This article is general information, not legal or regulatory advice, and it isn't a substitute for reading the Dental Council's own codes. If you're not sure how a rule applies to your practice, check the current code directly or speak to your professional indemnity provider or the Council itself.

Who actually regulates what a dentist can say on their website?

The Dental Council of Ireland, the statutory regulator for the dental profession under the Dentists Act 1985. It maintains the Register of Dentists and publishes a set of codes of practice that registered dentists are expected to follow, covering areas from clinical practice to professional conduct. A dental practice website falls squarely within "professional conduct" territory, alongside anything else the practice says publicly.

The safest starting point for any dentist building or updating a website isn't a blog post like this one — it's the current version of the Council's own code, since codes get reviewed and reissued from time to time and a summary can go out of date.

What does the Council's conduct code cover for advertising and communications?

The Council's Code of Practice on Professional Behaviour and Ethical Conduct sets the general standard registered dentists are expected to meet, and professional conduct rules of this kind typically expect anything a practice publishes — a website included — to be accurate and not misleading. That's a broader, more open-ended test than a simple checklist, which is exactly why it's worth reading the actual code rather than relying on a shortlist of dos and don'ts.

One thing that is straightforward: a dentist should only use professional titles and qualifications they actually hold and are entitled to under their registration. It's a basic principle across regulated healthcare professions in Ireland, not something specific to websites — but a website is often where it's most visible.

"The dentists who get this right treat their website the same way they'd treat anything printed and put in the waiting room — would I be comfortable with a patient, or the Council, reading this back to me? If the answer's yes, you're generally in good shape."

— Luke Stapleton, SuirViewDigital

Does a dentist have to display fees on their website?

Not specifically, no — and this is a common misconception worth clearing up. The Council's Display of Fees code requires fees to be shown physically in the practice — at the entrance, reception, or waiting area, on a notice at least A4 in size — rather than setting an online requirement. That said, plenty of practices choose to show indicative pricing on their website anyway, simply because it's a genuinely welcome piece of transparency for anyone researching before they call. If you're weighing that decision, it's worth reading the code itself first so you know exactly what "accurate and inclusive of all costs" means for a notice, since the same honesty standard is a sensible one to apply online even where it isn't strictly required there.

What about cosmetic treatments like teeth whitening or fillers?

These sit under their own separate rules. The Council publishes a specific code of practice on non-surgical cosmetic procedures, distinct from general dental treatment, and it's worth checking that code specifically rather than assuming the same wording that's fine for a routine filling automatically covers a cosmetic treatment page. This is exactly the kind of detail that's easy to get wrong by copying language from a general dental site template — our note on accessible website basics covers a related point: what's fine as a general default often needs a second look for a specific page.

What happens if a website doesn't meet the code?

Breaches of the Council's codes can, in principle, lead to fitness-to-practise proceedings — the Council's formal process for looking into concerns about a registered dentist's conduct. That's a strong enough consequence that it's worth treating website content with the same care as anything else published under the practice's name, and checking against the current code before publishing rather than after a concern is raised.

Where should you actually check before you publish?

Directly on dentalcouncil.ie, reading the current code yourself. It's a short, worthwhile task before a site goes live, in the same way it's worth checking a site is technically sound before launch — see our piece on whether the European Accessibility Act applies to your website for a similar "check the primary source, not a summary" approach to another compliance question small practices face.

Frequently asked questions

Who actually regulates what a dentist can say on their website in Ireland?

The Dental Council of Ireland, the statutory regulator for the profession under the Dentists Act 1985. It publishes codes of practice that dentists are expected to follow, including on professional and ethical conduct.

Does a dentist have to display fees on their website?

The Dental Council's Display of Fees code requires fees to be shown physically in the practice — at the entrance, reception or waiting area — not specifically on a website. Many practices choose to show indicative pricing online too, as good practice, but it isn't the same requirement.

Where can I read the actual rules myself before publishing my site?

Directly on dentalcouncil.ie, under Code of Practice. Read the current version yourself rather than relying on a summary, since codes are reviewed and updated.
