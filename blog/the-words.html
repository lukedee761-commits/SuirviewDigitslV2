03 — Content & copy

The words

The third layer · 5 min read

In short: Almost nobody is reading. They are scanning.

Most website copy is written as though someone will read it. Almost nobody does.

Scanning, not reading

Jakob Nielsen published the foundational study on this in 1997, and it has been replicated many times since. Watching users work through web pages, his team found the behaviour was not reading at all.

That is not laziness. It is a rational response to a medium where the next option is always one tap away. A visitor arrives with a specific question — do you cover my area, can you come this week, roughly what will it cost — and they are hunting for the answer, not reading your introduction.

Rewriting measurably works

The same research tested different versions of the same content against a control written in typical promotional style. Each change was measured for its effect on usability:

  • Concise text: 58% improvement
  • Scannable layout: 47% improvement
  • Objective language instead of marketing language: 27% improvement
  • All three combined: 124% improvement

The interesting result is the last one. The gains compound. Cutting the word count helps, formatting for scanning helps, and dropping the sales language helps — and doing all three more than doubles the measured result.

What it costs you

A beautiful page that persuades nobody. Every question you leave unanswered is a reason to close the tab and ring the next name on the list, and the questions are nearly always the same three: where, when, and how much.

What to actually write

Answer the questions in the order they are asked, in the words a customer would use. Not bespoke solutions tailored to your requirements. Rather: what you do, the areas you cover, how soon you can start, what it costs or what it depends on, and what happens after they get in touch.

Write it plainly, put the answer first and the explanation after, and use headings that say what is underneath them. A scanner should be able to skim your headings alone and come away with the substance.

The honest limitation

The core finding here dates from 1997 and the web has changed enormously since. What has not changed is the underlying behaviour — later eye-tracking work from the same group, including the F-pattern studies, found the same scanning habit persisting. Treat the exact percentages as historic, and the principle as current.
