02 — User interface

The interface

The second layer · 4 min read

In short: Most of your visitors are holding a phone in one hand.

The interface layer is the one people notice only when it fails. Nobody finishes a booking and thinks about navigation. They think about it when they cannot find the button, give up, and ring somebody else.

Where your visitors actually are

That is a global figure across all site types, so treat it as direction rather than gospel for a Tipperary salon — a local service business often skews further toward mobile than the average, because people look these things up while out. But the direction has been settled for years: more than half your visitors are on a small screen, one-handed, probably distracted.

What that changes

A desktop-first site that has been squeezed onto a phone is not a mobile site. The tells are consistent:

  • Phone numbers as plain text instead of tap-to-call links
  • Menus hidden behind an icon with no label, three taps from anything useful
  • Buttons sized for a mouse pointer rather than a thumb
  • Opening hours in a table that scrolls sideways off the screen
  • Forms that demand a postcode and a preferred contact time before anything happens

None of these are visible on a designer's monitor. All of them are the entire experience for the majority of your traffic.

What it costs you

They wanted to book. They could not find how. They left, and you never knew they were there — which is the particular cruelty of this layer. A broken interface produces silence, not complaints.

The test that matters

Open your own site on your own phone, standing up, with one hand, and try to do the single thing a customer most wants to do. Book. Ring. Ask a price. Count the taps. If it takes more than two, that is the number to fix.

Everything else in this layer is detail. Tap targets large enough to hit without aiming, a phone number that dials when touched, the primary action visible without scrolling, and a menu that names its destinations rather than hiding them.
