01 — Visual design

The look

The first layer · 5 min read

In short: The judgement made before a word is read.

There is a comfortable idea in business that looks do not matter much — that customers see past a dated website to the quality of the work behind it. The research does not support it, and it has not for twenty years.

Fifty milliseconds

In 2006, Gitte Lindgaard and colleagues at Carleton University ran three studies on how quickly people form a view of a web page. Participants were shown homepages for 500 milliseconds and asked to rate visual appeal. The third study repeated the test at 50 milliseconds — a twentieth of a second, barely longer than a blink.

The ratings held. Judgements made at 50 milliseconds correlated closely with those made at 500 milliseconds, and both were stable when the test was repeated. The conclusion the authors drew is the one that gave the paper its title: a designer has roughly fifty milliseconds to make a first impression.

That is not enough time to read your headline. It is not enough time to find your phone number, register that you are local, or notice that you have twenty years' experience. It is enough time to decide whether this looks like a real business.

Why it sticks

A snap judgement would not matter if it washed out once someone actually read the page. It does not. Four years before the Carleton study, B.J. Fogg's team at Stanford asked 2,684 people to assess the credibility of websites across ten categories and to say, in their own words, why.

Design look was mentioned more often than any other factor, ahead of information structure and information focus. People said they were judging trustworthiness, and what they described was the visual treatment. The surface was standing in for everything underneath it.

What it costs you

A visitor who decides in fifty milliseconds that a site looks dated has already priced the business behind it. They are not comparing your work to your competitor's work. They are comparing your website to their website, and then assuming the rest follows.

What this layer actually is

The look is not decoration and it is not taste. It is a set of decisions that a visitor reads as competence within a fraction of a second:

  • Consistent spacing, so the page reads as built rather than assembled
  • A type system with a deliberate hierarchy, not three fonts fighting each other
  • Photography of the actual business, at a resolution that holds up on a modern screen
  • A colour palette that stays the same from the header to the footer
  • Alignment that holds on a phone as well as it does on a laptop

None of that is expensive to get right at the start. All of it is expensive to retrofit, because by the time it is obviously wrong, the site has usually grown around the problem.

The honest limitation

Both studies above measured perception, not sales. Neither followed participants to a purchase, and neither was run on Irish local-business websites. What they establish is narrower and still useful: the visual layer is judged almost instantly, that judgement is stable, and people use it as a proxy for whether a business can be trusted.

Which is the whole argument for this layer. It is the cheapest trust you will ever buy — and it is the only layer most cheap websites actually deliver.
